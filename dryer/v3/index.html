Version 3: solar canopy machine

Tip in crates of fresh produce; take out dried, sorted product. One machine on wheels with a clip-on wash module and a solar canopy roof. About 4.4 × 1.8 m and 2.6 m tall.

Open the 3D prototype

What it does, in one line

Wash, sort, cut, dry and store up to six kinds of produce at once, each at its own temperature, heated only by the sun. No generator, no fuel.

How it works

  1. WashThe farmer tips a crate into a shallow water tank. A paddle pushes produce onto a brushing belt under water sprays, then a fan blows off the water.
  2. Line up and lookA vibrating channel lines pieces up under a camera. The camera names each piece and checks its quality.
  3. SortGates send damaged pieces to the reject bin. If there’s no free tray for a produce, it goes to the overflow bin for the next batch.
  4. CutA spinning blade slices or halves each piece as its recipe says. Leaves and small peppers skip this.
  5. LoadCut pieces drop into a small carriage. It climbs to its tray, and a vibrating spreader swings across the belt while the belt creeps forward, laying one even layer. One crate fills one tray, then the carriage goes back for the next.
  6. Catch the sunThe sloped canopy roof is the heater: half is glass vacuum tubes, half is a black zig-zag sheet under clear glazing that air passes over twice. Both work on hazy days too. The canopy also shades the doors and keeps off rain. Hot air flows down into a wall of wax tubes that store heat for the night.
  7. DryThree zones, each at its own temperature. Belts jog under a fixed comb to turn the pieces. Sensors weigh every tray. The AI sends warm air round again while it’s still dry and vents it when it’s humid. At night a silica gel bed keeps drying the air with no heat at all; the next day’s sun dries the gel again.
  8. UnloadWhen a tray is dry, a second carriage stops just under its end. The belt runs and the product drops a few centimetres into the carriage’s trough. The carriage moves to that produce’s drawer, a hatch opens, and the trough tips in.
  9. StoreSix sealed drawers watch humidity and CO₂. The operator bags product when the screen says a drawer is full.

How air flows

  1. In at the frontOutside air enters along the low front edge of the canopy, about 2.2 m up, through a washable mesh filter.
  2. Heated twiceIt splits to flow over and under the black zig-zag sheet, rising up the slope to the back, while the vacuum tubes heat their share.
  3. Down to the heat wallFrom the header at the high back edge, a duct drops the hot air into the wax heat wall, then the zone fans push it across the trays.
  4. Out at the backMoist air leaves through the chimney at the back, or the AI sends it round again while it’s still dry.
  5. Closed at nightWhen there’s no heat left, the intake and zone dampers close together so humid night air stays out; the silica gel keeps drying.

Why the intake is at the low front: hot air rises, so air entering low and leaving high moves the way the fans pull it. The intake is far from the wet exhaust at the back, high above ground dust and wash splashes, and sheltered from rain by the canopy overhang.

Electrical system

The heat is all solar-thermal. Electricity only runs fans, motors and electronics, from its own solar panel:

  1. Solar panel (300 W)On the wash module roof. Charges the battery through a charge controller (MPPT).
  2. Battery (12 V 100 Ah lithium)In the ventilated base. About 1.15 kWh usable, enough for a full night and a cloudy day.
  3. Fuse boardEvery branch has its own fuse: fans, belt motors, carriages, wash equipment, computers.
  4. ControllersA main controller, one small node per zone, motor drivers and a relay board for the wash equipment. A small computer runs the AI and the camera.
  5. SafetyThe emergency stop cuts all motor power through its own line.

Daily need is about 1.1 kWh with AI control; a 300 W panel gives about 1.3 kWh on a sunny day. In the 3D model, switch View to “Electrical” to see the wiring and live power flow.

What the operator does

  • Tip crates into the wash tank, one crate (one produce) at a time.
  • Empty the reject bin; put overflow back in next batch.
  • Drain and refill wash water daily.
  • Bag full drawers; clean weekly.

Produce it handles

Only produce that needs no peeling, so everything stays automatic. A zone holds one temperature group; each of its two trays can hold a different produce from that group.

GroupTemperatureProduce
Leavesabout 40–45 °CMoringa, ugwu, scent leaf, bitter leaf, ewedu, tete, uziza
Vegetablesabout 50–58 °CTomato, tatashe, rodo, okra, garden egg, carrot, zobo, ginger, mushroom
Starchy chipsabout 65 °CSweet potato, Irish potato (skin on)

Temperatures are starting points to confirm in tests. Yam, plantain and cassava are left out because they need peeling. Onion, garlic and fish are left out because their smell taints other produce.

Why we added things beyond the brief

AdditionWhy it’s needed
WashingSoil and mould spores on unwashed produce spoil the dried product and raise aflatoxin risk. Automatic washing makes it safe to sell and saves the farmer’s hardest job.
Sorting channelThe camera can only judge one piece at a time, so pieces must be lined up for it to reject damaged ones and route each produce.
SlicerWhole tomatoes and peppers take a week or more and can rot inside. Even slices dry in hours to a day or two, and dry evenly.
Belt trays and carriagesFixed trays need a person to load and empty them. Belts and carriages make loading and unloading automatic.
Vacuum tubes instead of the mirror dishThe dish uses only direct sun, which haze and cloud cut sharply in Ibadan: about 10 days per batch in harmattan in our model. Tubes and the roof collector also use scattered light: about 29 hours. No tracking motors or 4.3 m mast, cheaper and safer in wind.
Small solar panelHeating stays 100% solar-thermal as briefed; the panel never heats. Fans, motors, sensors, AI and camera need electricity, and most farms have no grid. A small panel and battery is the only fuel-free way to run them.

How the work was redistributed

When Olagoke Oluwaseyi (ME4) joined, the work was shared out again. The goal was not just equal amounts of work, but scopes that match each student’s project title, so everyone can design, build, test and write a full thesis on their own system while it still fits into one machine.

The rules we followed

  • Title first. Each person’s core work comes straight from their project title.
  • One owner per part. No part is shared. Where two systems meet, we define the hand-over instead of splitting ownership.
  • Structure vs. function. Seyi builds the frames things sit on; the owner of each system keeps its design, performance and testing.
  • New work goes where it fits. Washing was in nobody’s title, so it became the functional half of Seyi’s new topic.

What changed and why

She takes the main frame, base, canopy supports, storage cabinet and the wash module, plus the full AutoCAD drawings everyone builds from. This uses her CAD strength and gives her a real working subsystem, not only drawings.
  • ME1, Jamiat Opeyemi Akande. Her title is the solar collector and PCM storage, so she keeps all of that: roof collector, tubes, air intake and wax wall. Only the canopy support frame moves to Seyi.
  • ME2, Obaloluwa Vincent Ayeni. His title is the multi-zone chamber and CFD, so the chamber, fans, dampers, recirculation ducts and silica gel bed stay. Deciding when to recirculate moves to the AI (MEE3), because that’s a control decision, not airflow design.
  • ME3, Beloved Oyebode Adeoye. His title is agitation and redistribution, so he keeps the belt trays, combs and the spreader that lays an even layer. The carriages that move produce go to MEE5, whose title is handling.

  • MEE2, Mayowa Samuel Elegbede. Embedded control and safety. He keeps the whole electrical system. The technical bay’s steel structure goes to Seyi and the touchscreen software to MEE6.
  • MEE3, Oluwatoyin Muftaudeen Jimoh. AI adaptive control for energy-efficient drying. He gains recirculation decisions and energy budgeting, so every decision lives in one place.
  • MEE4, Olatunde Akolade Salako. Vision for quality assessment and classification. He gains a second camera that grades the dried product, so quality is checked at the output as well as the intake.
  • MEE5, Praise Timilehin Olumuyiwa. Automated handling and sorting. The wash module goes to Seyi, and he gains full ownership of both carriages, so everything that moves produce is his.
  • MEE6, Babatunde Muhibudeen Raji. IoT monitoring and predictive storage. He gains the touchscreen software and the digital twin (this 3D site), which fit “digital monitoring”. The cabinet structure goes to Seyi.
